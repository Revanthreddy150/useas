Self-Reflection: Review past concepts

Review past concepts
Part 1: Relationship between the data life cycle and the data analysis process Both frameworks guide how data is handled to drive informed decisions. They are similar because they both follow a structured, sequential progression. They differ because the data life cycle covers the entire lifespan of data from creation to destruction, whereas the data analysis process focuses specifically on solving a problem through analysis. Part 2: Relationship between the Ask phase and the Plan stage Both the Ask phase of data analysis and the Plan stage of the data life cycle focus on defining goals and expectations at the start of a project. They are similar in that they establish project scope and identify data needs. They differ because the Ask phase centers on defining the business problem, while the Plan stage focuses on deciding how data will be gathered, managed, and structured throughout its lifecycle.

Self-Reflection: Business use of data

1. How could it use data to improve customer experience?

An e-commerce platform can analyze customer browsing history and past purchases to provide personalized product recommendations. By understanding individual preferences, the platform streamlines the shopping journey, making it faster and more relevant for every user.

2. What kinds of data would it need to collect?

The platform needs to collect user interaction metrics such as clickstream logs, search queries, cart additions, and completed purchase histories. Additionally, it should gather customer feedback ratings and device usage details to comprehensively understand overall user behavior patterns and preferences.

3. How could insights from that data solve a problem?

Analyzing purchase drop-off points can reveal why customers frequently abandon their shopping carts, allowing the company to simplify the checkout process. Resolving this specific friction point directly reduces cart abandonment rates and significantly increases overall sales conversion for the business.

Self-Reflection: Business cases

Question 1
Examples of fair or unfair practices: A major unfair practice is self-selection bias, since participation was voluntary and more motivated or proactive teachers were likely the ones who attended. Additionally, attributing the score difference entirely to the workshop without establishing a pre-workshop baseline creates an unfair and invalid causal conclusion. How could a data analyst correct the unfair practices: A data analyst could correct this by conducting a pre- and post-workshop evaluation to measure individual improvement rather than comparing raw post-workshop scores. They could also implement a randomized controlled trial or use regression analysis to control for confounding variables like prior teaching experience.
Question 2
Examples of fair or unfair practices: A fair practice is testing across multiple roadway surfaces, such as dirt roads and race tracks, while collecting both sensor and video data. However, an unfair practice is sampling bias from testing exclusively during the daytime, which ignores low-light vision and night driving conditions. How could a data analyst correct the unfair practices: A data analyst could correct this by expanding the test protocol to collect data across diverse lighting conditions, including dawn, dusk, and nighttime. They should also establish safety benchmarks across every condition before concluding that the car is ready for subsequent phases.
Question 3
Examples of fair or unfair practices: A fair practice is surveying actual park visitors directly to gather user feedback before investing in costly new rides. However, an unfair practice is significant sampling bias from surveying only guests queued for roller coasters. This convenience sampling systematically overrepresents thrill-seekers while entirely excluding families, children, and visitors at other attractions. How could a data analyst correct the unfair practices: A data analyst could correct this unfair practice by implementing stratified random sampling across various park locations, including family areas, dining zones, and main exits. They could also collect responses through digital post-visit surveys sent via email or the park mobile app. This approach ensures balanced representation across all visitor demographics.
